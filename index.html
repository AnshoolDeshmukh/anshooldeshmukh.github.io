Anshool Deshmukh is a Toronto-based communications professional with 9 years of experience turning complex ideas into content that actually connects. With a Master's in Journalism from the Grady College of Journalism and Mass Communication and a foundation in computer science, he sits comfortably at the intersection of storytelling, strategy, and technology.

Over the years, Anshool has led communications for some of Canada's most recognized organizations, from global pharmaceutical giants like Sanofi to mission-driven nonprofits like BGC Canada, with stops in healthcare, real estate, and financial content along the way. His work spans internal communications, brand strategy, editorial governance, digital content, and everything in between. If it needs to be written, structured, or made to land with an audience, he's the person in the room.

He's also a firm believer that good communication isn't just about what you say. It's about who you're saying it to, why it matters to them, and whether it moves them to do something about it.

When he's not crafting communication strategies or obsessing over a well-placed comma, you'll find him hunting down Toronto's best new restaurants and breweries, curating the perfect playlist nobody asked for, or stopping to pet every dog he passes. A confessed crime thriller addict, former co-host of a space exploration podcast, currently building a healthcare education podcast, and somewhere in between all of that, writing his first novel, because apparently, one form of storytelling was never going to be enough.

The Brief

BGC Canada needed Canadians to feel the cost of growing up without support. Not through statistics. Not through charity speak. Through the specific, quiet moments that kids carry alone because there's nowhere safe to share them.

The brief landed with a deceptively simple ask: make people feel something. The harder question was how to earn that feeling without manufacturing it.

The Idea

"Kids shouldn't have unshareable stories."

Simple. The weight comes from what it doesn't say. It doesn't lecture. It doesn't list statistics. It trusts the audience to feel the gap between what a childhood should look like and what too many kids actually experience.

The line needed to work across every issue BGC touches — bullying, hunger, poverty, gender identity, immigration. It does, because the structure never changes. Only the story does.

The Execution

Wrote and shaped the alumni narratives featured on the campaign hub. Damion, Daniela, Kowmitha, Teresa. Each story translated a real person's experience into copy that was emotionally honest without being exploitative, holding the same standard of dignity the campaign demanded throughout.

Bullying TVC — Direction Note

Raw, observational, documentary-style. The cruelty is casual, not cartoonish. The sound of the book hitting the ground acts as the dialogue of the scene. It needs to cut through the ambience. That casual quality makes it more impactful than anything an actor could say.

Hunger TVC — Direction Note

Natural, intimate, documentary-inspired. The locker POV creates immediate claustrophobia and privacy. We're inside his secret with him. The dialogue does the work. Nobody believes him. He knows it.

The social campaign introduced real kids by name, told their story in one breath, and landed on the same line every time. No call to action. No elaborate hashtag strategy. Just the story and the line. The restraint is the craft.

Why It Worked

The brief could have produced a campaign that made people feel guilty. It didn't. It made them feel something more useful: recognition. Every adult has been one of these kids, or knows one.

The copy never told audiences what to think. It put them inside a specific moment, with a specific child, and trusted them to arrive at the feeling themselves. That's the difference between advertising that runs and advertising that stays with people.

The Brief

Mattamy Homes is one of Canada's largest homebuilders, operating across communities from Ontario to Alberta. The brief was deceptively simple: write the website, the community pages, and the social campaigns.

The real challenge was harder. A home is the biggest purchase most people ever make. The copy couldn't just describe square footage and highway access. It had to make someone feel like they already belonged there before they ever set foot inside.

The Idea

"Not a builder's voice. A buyer's voice."

Every word had to work across buyer types, price points, and communities with completely different characters, while sounding like it came from one voice. Make people feel something before they ever walk through a door.

The Execution

The homepage needed to answer one question immediately: why Mattamy? The answer wasn't product features. It was philosophy. That opening repositions Mattamy from builder to partner. Everything else on the site flows from that promise.

Community Page — Hawthorne East Village

"Celebrate each day in Mattamy's Hawthorne East Village, with its picturesque homes and everything your active family needs to grow and thrive. By the end, the reader isn't buying a house. They're buying a version of their life."

The Design Studio page solved a specific problem: personalization is exciting in theory and paralyzing in practice. The copy had to make hundreds of decisions feel like a privilege, not a burden. One page. Two buyer types. Neither one left without a next step.

This approach was applied across Mattamy's entire Canadian portfolio. Every community has its own narrative. One brand voice throughout.

Why It Worked

Real estate copy fails when it describes. It succeeds when it imagines. The brief required writing that could make someone picture Sunday morning coffee on a back porch in a community that didn't exist yet.

The copy that performed best was the copy that trusted the reader, gave them just enough to see themselves there, and let their imagination do the rest.

The Brief

BGC Canada wanted to create a national day of celebration for its 600+ Clubs and the kids, families, and communities they serve. A feel-good charity day wasn't enough. It needed to feel like an event people genuinely wanted to be part of, not just something they were asked to support.

The Idea

"Turn a national nonprofit into a national celebration."

Bring in partners with genuine cultural reach. Light up the country in BGC green. Give people a reason to show up, share, and pay attention. Make BGC impossible to ignore for one day.

The Execution

Wrote all event copy and campaign messaging across digital, social, and partner communications, from the initial awareness push through to event day content, maintaining a voice that was warm, energetic, and community-first throughout.

Social Formula — The Club Day Story

"Having dedicated over 7 years to @bgc_es, Shintelle's Club journey has been inspiring. From After School Program Lead to Youth Program Coordinator to Social Media Specialist, she's impacted countless young lives. Join us on June 2 to celebrate #BGCClubDay."

The formula was consistent: a real person, a real journey, the same invitation. Personal enough to stop the scroll. Specific enough to be believed.

Walk Off the Earth performed live on BGC's Facebook. CityTV and CBC Evening News covered Jully Black's visit to BGC East Scarborough. 41 civic activations across Canada, including 5 municipal proclamations, BGC flags raised at 11 City Halls, and 25 landmarks lit in BGC green — the CN Tower, Niagara Falls, the Montreal Olympic Stadium.

Why It Worked

The brief asked for awareness. The campaign delivered credibility. Earned media, organic celebrity amplification, civic buy-in from 11 city halls, and a country turned green aren't things you buy. They're things you earn when the creative and the cause are strong enough that people want to be associated with them.

Club Day proved BGC wasn't just a charity. It was a cultural institution worth celebrating.
